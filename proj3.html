Overview

This project explores going from random noise to a data distribution (in this case natural images) using a learned network. Specifically I look at flow matching which effectively tries to learn a velocity field from source noise to my desired data distribution which in this case is images.

Part A: Exploring the Power of Flow Models

1. Visualizing Training and Sampling

I linearly interpolate between noise and data by sampling noise ε=x0 from a zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian, obtaining some clean image x1, and then constructing my linearly interpolated image xt as

(1−t)x0+tx1,(1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,

where t is between 0 and 1. At train time, I sample a clean image, some noise, and t from a uniform distribution and construct xt. The model is then conditioned on the constructed xt, sampled t, and desired conditioning. The model is then tasked with predicting the velocity from noise to data (x1−x0) and is trained with the objective

ℒ(θ)=𝔼[‖vθ(xt,t,c)−(x1−x0)‖22]\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t, c) - (x_1 - x_0)\rVert_2^2\right]

1.5 Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

Instead of doing one-step denoising, I can predict the velocity at a point, move a small amount in that direction, and then predict the velocity at the slightly denoised image. I repeat this until t=1. This process is called Euler integration and can be formulated as

xt+h=xt+hvθ(xt,t,c).x_{t+h} = x_t + h\,v_\theta(x_t, t, c).

Compared to one-step denoising, where I multiply the velocity by (1−t), I multiply by a fixed delta h.

Comparison

Gaussian blur0.0478

All three start from the t = 0.50 noisy input. MSE is averaged over every pixel and RGB channel, with pixel values in [−1, 1].

The Gaussian blur denoising method reduces the “noisiness” of the image but leaves artifacts of the noise as well as also destroying high-frequency details. Since the model generating velocities was trained with an L2-based objective, the one-step estimate approximates the mean, which averages over all plausible clean images and causes some smoothing, which discards some detail (notably the dark structures in the water). This also explains why the one-step estimate has a smaller MSE even though the iterative Euler-based denoising qualitatively looks much closer to the original image.

2. Conditional Generation and Guidance

2.1 Classifier-Free Guidance

CFG helps with the quality of generations by artificially pushing the model’s predictions away from what it would predict without the prompt. At each sampling step, I first compute a conditional velocity vc (with the prompt) and an unconditional velocity vu (with the empty prompt), both at the same xt and t. I then push the conditional velocity further away from the unconditional one by adding w times their difference.

vcfg=vc+w(vc−vu) v_\text{cfg} = v_c + w\,(v_c - v_u)

vc velocity with the promptvu velocity with the empty prompt “”w = 7

3.3 Visual Anagrams

I want to make generations that are anagrams. Right-side up, the image will reveal something, and then upside down, the image will reveal something else. To do this, I augment the velocity. I first compute two velocities v1, v2 as

v1=CFG velocity(xt,t,prompt1) v2=flip(CFG velocity(flip(xt),t,prompt2))

and then the final velocity I use is calculated as

v=v1+v22.

3.4 Hybrid Images

For generating hybrid images that look like one image up close and another image from far away, I employ a similar trick as the anagrams by modifying the velocity. Here I calculate velocities for each of my prompts, v1 and v2, as

v1=CFG velocity(xt,t,prompt1), v2=CFG velocity(xt,t,prompt2).

Then I pass v1 through a low-pass filter (which will express v1 when we are far away) and pass v2 through a high-pass filter (which will express v2 when we are close up). The final velocity is

v=flowpass(v1)+fhighpass(v2),

where the filters are implemented as

flowpass(v)=G∗v, fhighpass(v)=v−flowpass(v),

The distant view is not a model output: the hybrid was downsampled to 32 × 32 px, keeping only its low frequencies, then upsampled back to 512 × 512 so it can be seen at full size. The small copy is the unaltered hybrid shown at 48 px.

2. Training a Flow Matching Model

2.1 Adding Time Conditioning to UNet

I modify my original UNet architecture to condition on how noisy the noised image is with t. The network uses fully connected blocks (FCBs) to map t to one scale per channel: 1 → 128 for the 128×7×7 Unflatten output and 1 → 64 for the 64×14×14 output of the first UpBlock. Each is multiplied in before the skip connection is concatenated.

The only problem here is that the output number is a function of the input noise, so there’s no way to control it, so I can introduce class conditioning to try and get the model to generate numbers I want regardless of the input noise.

2.4 Adding Class-Conditioning to UNet

I take the time-conditioned UNet and modify it to accept a one-hot encoded class vector of what single-digit number I want it to generate. At the same conditioning locations, instead of multiplying by the projected t block, I multiply by the projected c block and add the t block.

Removing the Learning-Rate Scheduler

If I want to remove the learning rate scheduler for the sake of simplicity and don’t want to overshoot the optimum, I need to use a smaller learning rate, but a smaller learning rate takes longer to converge, so I also need to increase the number of epochs I train for. I go from an initial LR of 1e-2 with a 0.1× decay over 10 epochs to a constant 1e-3 LR that’s trained for 20 epochs.

3. Bells & Whistles

CIFAR-10

CIFAR-10 is a dataset of 60,000 32×32 color images split evenly across 10 classes: airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship and truck. 50,000 images are for training and 10,000 for testing; I train on the 50,000. Compared to MNIST, the images are in color, slightly larger, and far more varied in pose, background and lighting.

Time- and Class-Conditioned UNet

I made the following small modifications to the MNIST Time- and Class-Conditioned UNet:

  • Switched from 1-channel 28×28 inputs to 3-channel 32×32 inputs, since CIFAR is RGB and has slightly higher dimensions.
  • To accommodate the new dimensions and preserve the 1/2 downsampling ratio, I adjust the spatial sizes from 28 → 14 → 7 → 1 to 32 → 16 → 8 → 1 and, as a result, also change the average pooling and conv transpose operations to match those dimensions.
  • I also use a hidden width of 128 to accommodate the higher complexity of the data.

Parameters 9.0MEpochs 100

Wider and Deeper UNet

I wasn’t satisfied with the generations of the previous architecture, so I introduced 3 major changes:

  1. Introduced two residual blocks at every resolution instead of one convolutional block. This allows for more expressive representations in the network, and it still trains well due to the presence of skip connections within the block itself, which help gradients flow.[1]
  2. Instead of pooling the 8×8 map into 1×1 and then blowing it back up with the conv transpose, I use a self-attention bottleneck that keeps it at 8×8 and allows it to get global spatial information by letting each position attend to every other position. It’s implemented with a residual block, which then goes through a multi-head self-attention layer and then into another residual block.[1]
  3. I insert time and class conditioning into every residual block, so the network is hyper-aware of the current noise level and the desired class to generate.[2]

Finally, due to the presence of all the new layers and increased number of feature maps, the network is much deeper & wider and can hopefully learn to make much better generations.

Parameters 21.0MEpochs 100

The generations using this deeper architecture slightly outperform the original architecture when trained for 100 epochs. Training for longer would likely yield better results.
