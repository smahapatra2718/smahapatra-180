Boundary handling

The boundary conditions depend on the type of convolution. For full convolution, I take the filter size (fh,fw) and then add fw−1 columns of zeros on the left and right side of the image, respectively, and then I add fh−1 rows of zeros to the top and bottom of the image, respectively. The final dimension of the input image is (imgh+2(fh−1))×(imgw+2(fw−1)). The output is size (imgh+fh−1)×(imgw+fw−1). If the mode is same, we just perform the full convolution and then slice the extra padding off the sides to produce something that matches the size of the input.

Part 2.1: Image “Sharpening”

Unsharp masking essentially works to boost the high frequencies in the image. Conceptually, we take an image, convolve it with a Gaussian to obtain the low frequencies, which we can then subtract from the original image to obtain the high frequencies. We then take those high frequencies and boost them by multiplying by some α parameter. We can then add this boosted high-frequency image back to the original to get an ending result that appears sharper. Practically, this operation can be condensed into one filter that we can convolve with our source image by using the unit impulse δ. The filter is defined as (1+α)δ−αG, which, if expanded when doing convolution, results in an operation exactly equivalent to what was described above.

Taj Mahal

Oakland

Reversing a blur with the unsharp filter

The unsharp masking process doesn’t actually introduce or recover any information; it just pushes pixels away from the average of their neighbors. In low-frequency areas this doesn’t really change anything, but in high-frequency areas it can make details such as edges, textures, and noise more prominent. In the Taj Mahal photograph, the bricks, scaffolding, and outline become much more defined as we boost α. In the Oakland scene, one noticeable detail is the grain of the rock on the right side. With more and more sharpening, it looks like more aggressive noise. This image is also interesting because there are many straight-line edges that get noticeably more defined as you increase α. Finally, if we apply a destructive blur to the Oakland image and then sharpen it, we can get a result that is visually close to the original, albeit with some blurring.

Part 2.2: Hybrid Images

This section explores an effect where a viewer sees one image up close and a different one from far away. The core mechanism behind this effect is that high and low frequencies are perceived differently at different viewing distances. If we filter one of our two component images with a low-pass filter and the other with a high-pass filter (the original image minus its low-pass version) and then combine them, we get exactly that result. At close range the high-pass details become apparent, but at greater distances those frequencies seem to disappear and only the low-pass image remains visible.

Should hybrid images be in color?

When choosing which components of the hybrid should have color, the first significant factor to consider is that humans are inherently not that sensitive to high frequencies in color compared with grayscale. This becomes apparent when comparing pairs of images that differ only in whether the high-pass component is colored: “Both gray” and “Color high-pass” are almost indistinguishable, as are “Color low-pass” and “Both color.” In that sense, the key distinction is whether or not the low-pass image should be colored. I personally believe that adding color to the low-pass image can be quite beneficial, especially if the images are well aligned in terms of color, as can be seen in the Day + Night and Sunflower + Lion hybrids. One caveat is that when color is used for the low-pass image, it can dominate the high-pass image even at close range, so it can sometimes be beneficial to keep the low-pass image grayscale to preserve the effect of the hybrid.

Part 2.3 + 2.4: Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks + Multiresolution Blending

This section explores how to elegantly blend two images based on a mask without jarring transitions. First, we make a Gaussian stack for each image, progressively blurring it more and more while preserving its size. Then we construct a Laplacian stack, where Li=Gi−Gi+1, which captures the band of frequencies between consecutive Gaussian levels. L0 holds the highest frequencies, and the last Laplacian level holds the lowest (it is simply set to the last Gaussian level). The Laplacian stack is a lossless decomposition of the image into frequency bands, as can be seen from the fact that adding up the stack recovers the original image. We also construct a Gaussian stack of the mask we want to use. Then we step through the two images’ Laplacian stacks and the mask’s Gaussian stack level by level, forming triplets. At each level, we apply the mask to blend the two bands, GiMLiA+(1−GiM)LiB, and then we sum the blended levels back together to create the final image.

Conclusion

This project was super interesting in terms of fundamentally understanding how frequencies are an extremely important factor to consider when working with images. The first part allowed me to build solid intuitions behind convolution and apply it to the task of edge detection. It was also informative to see that removing noise from an image with a low-pass filter can greatly improve edge detection. The second part dove even further into the idea of manipulating different frequency bands to produce interesting-looking images. It’s quite interesting to see how manipulating the expression of different frequencies in images, or combinations of images, can produce better-quality images than just taking naive approaches. The most important thing I learned from this project is that working with images in the frequency domain can be quite useful.
